PTE's Write Essay task gives you a prompt, 20 minutes and a word counter. It looks simple, but small things, like going under 200 words or drifting off topic, can wipe out marks fast. A fixed structure solves most of it.

## How the essay is scored

Pearson scores the essay on several traits, including:

- **Content:** do you address the prompt fully and directly?
- **Form:** is it between 200 and 300 words, in proper sentences and paragraphs?
- **Development, structure and coherence:** is it well organised, with a clear argument?
- **Grammar, vocabulary range and general linguistic range.**
- **Spelling.**

Form and content act as gatekeepers. An essay that's off topic or outside the word limit can score very low however good the English is.

## A structure that fits the limit

Aim for about **250 words** in four paragraphs:

| Paragraph | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | ~40 words | Rephrase the topic and state your position |
| Body 1 | ~80 words | First main reason, explained, with an example |
| Body 2 | ~80 words | Second main reason, explained, with an example |
| Conclusion | ~40 words | Restate your position and sum up the reasons |

250 words leaves a safe margin on both sides of the limit.

## Use your 20 minutes like this

- **3 minutes:** read the prompt carefully and plan. Write your position and one idea for each body paragraph.
- **14 minutes:** write.
- **3 minutes:** check spelling, grammar and the word count.

## Answer the actual prompt

PTE prompts often have two parts: "Do you agree? What are the advantages?" Answer both. Use the prompt's key idea in your introduction and conclusion so the connection is obvious, but rephrase it rather than copying it word for word.

## Memorised templates are a risk

For years, students memorised long template paragraphs and fitted any topic into them. Since August 2025, PTE scoring combines AI with human review, and **memorised content is detected and penalised**.

What still works is a *structure*: the four paragraphs above, with your own sentences in each. Knowing a handful of linking phrases (*On the other hand*, *For instance*, *As a result*) is fine; whole memorised sentences are not.

## Write clearly, not fancily

- Use one idea per paragraph.
- Mix simple and complex sentences.
- Choose words you're sure of. A misused advanced word costs more than a correct common one.
- Use consistent spelling: if you write *organise*, don't later write *organize*.

## Common mistakes

- Writing under 200 or over 300 words.
- Writing one long block of text without paragraphs.
- Writing all in capital letters, or with no punctuation.
- Giving opinions that change halfway through.
- Long memorised sentences that don't fit the prompt.

## A practice plan

Write two essays a week under a strict 20-minute timer. After each one, check the word count, read it aloud to catch grammar slips, and list your spelling mistakes. Re-write your weakest paragraph. Within a month, the structure becomes automatic and you'll have more time for ideas.
